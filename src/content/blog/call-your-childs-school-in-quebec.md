## Why the office answers in French

This is not a receptionist being difficult. It is the law, and knowing that changes how you plan.

Quebec's Charter of the French Language, as amended in 2022, requires agencies of the civil administration to use French in an exemplary manner. Specifically, an agency uses French exemplarily if, in all its activities, it uses **exclusively** that language in its oral communications, except in the narrow cases where the Charter itself gives the option to use another language [B96]. The Office québécois de la langue française's guidance for school organisations lists exactly which situations fall under that rule — reception and telephone service, parent meetings, public talks, recorded phone messages and voicemail among them — and instructs school bodies to confirm French is not possible before relying on any exception [OQLF].

So: a French-language school service centre will answer the phone in French, run the parent-teacher meeting in French, and record its voicemail in French. Separately, English-language school service centres are recognised school bodies and may use the language of instruction in communications connected with teaching [CHARTER].

You are also not alone in this. In the 2021 Census, 47.3% of Quebec residents reported being able to hold a conversation in French only, and 82,075 people reported knowing neither English nor French [SC].

## The calls that actually come up

Almost none of them are complicated. That is what makes postponing them so expensive.

| The call | Typical length | What goes wrong when you wait |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporting an absence | 1–2 min | Unexcused absence on the record |
| Changing pickup or bus arrangements | 2–3 min | A child waiting somewhere alone |
| Booking a parent-teacher meeting | 2–4 min | Slots fill; you get the last one of the term |
| A missing form or signature | 2 min | Exclusion from a trip or activity |
| Asking for extra help or an assessment | 5–10 min | A full term passes before anything starts |

## Prepare on paper before you dial

Ninety per cent of the difficulty disappears if the facts exist in writing before the call starts. Write these out:

1. Your child's **full legal name** as the school has it, and date of birth.
2. **School name**, grade, and the **teacher's name**.
3. The **exact dates** involved ("absent Monday 14 and Tuesday 15").
4. **One sentence** stating what you need. Not context — the request.
5. Your genuine availability, as specific days and times.
6. Who you spoke to last time, and when.

And decide in advance what you must leave the call holding: a date, a time, a room number, a name, or a confirmation that something was recorded.

## Four ways to make the call anyway

**1. Ask the school, in writing, in advance.** Email the office a few days before a meeting and ask whether a staff member who speaks your language, or a community interpreter, can be present. There is no general Quebec statute obliging them to provide one, but many schools will try, and an early written request is far more likely to succeed than one made at the door. Keep the email.

**2. Bring a bilingual adult you trust.** Fine for logistics. Weaker for anything sensitive about your child's progress or behaviour, and it puts a relative in the middle of your family's business.

**3. Use written channels for anything non-urgent.** Email and the school portal give you time to compose and a record afterwards. Write short, plain, formatted French — three lines with the child's name, the dates and the request — rather than long prose in another language.

**4. Use a phone interpreter for the live calls.** For the two-minute calls, this is the practical answer. A traditional over-the-phone interpreting service puts a human on the line and bills by the minute. An AI call interpreter like Hello Dulce works differently: you write your message in Spanish, Arabic, Mandarin or your own language; it dials the school and says it in natural Quebec French; every sentence the secretary says comes back to you as text in your language while the call is still live; and at the end you keep the date, the room number and the name, written down.

## What to say: sentences worth having ready

Even if an interpreter is doing the talking, knowing the shape of the exchange helps. Useful French openers:

- *« Bonjour, je suis le parent de [nom de l'enfant], en [niveau]. »* — Hello, I am the parent of [child], in [grade].
- *« J'aimerais prendre rendez-vous avec l'enseignante de mon fils / ma fille. »* — I would like to book a meeting with my son's / daughter's teacher.
- *« Mon enfant sera absent le [date] pour un rendez-vous médical. »* — My child will be absent on [date] for a medical appointment.
- *« Pouvez-vous me confirmer cela par courriel, s'il vous plaît? »* — Could you confirm that by email, please?
- *« Pouvez-vous répéter plus lentement, s'il vous plaît? »* — Could you repeat that more slowly, please?

That fourth one is the most valuable sentence on the list. A written confirmation removes the entire language problem from everything that happens after the call.

## The parent-teacher meeting itself

- Send your questions in advance, in writing. Three at most, in priority order.
- Ask for the meeting's outcome in writing: what will change, who does it, by when.
- If an assessment or extra support is discussed, ask what the next formal step is and who initiates it.
- Take notes in your own language. Nobody will mind.

## The point

The barrier is not that Quebec schools are closed to you. It is that a two-minute call in a language you do not speak becomes a week-long project involving three other people — and some of those calls simply never get made. Put the facts on paper, insist on written confirmation, and pick a way to have the live conversation that does not depend on a relative being free at 9 a.m.
